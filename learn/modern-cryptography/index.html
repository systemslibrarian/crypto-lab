Chapter 1 · Foundations

Decide what “secure” should mean

Direct coverage

Historical encryption and the modern security framework

Reading pointers: 1.2–1.4

  • Vigenère BreakDirect coverage

    Use frequency and repeated-pattern evidence to challenge a classical cipher.

  • Hidden BitIntroductory coverage

    Preview the challenger, adversary and success condition that later chapters make precise.

Try this: Use a supplied challenge encrypted under a short repeating key. Before opening Hidden Bit, write down what the attacker sees, what the attacker may do, and what would count as success.

Explain the result: Why is “I cannot read this ciphertext” a weaker claim than a definition covering every efficient adversary?

Coverage boundary: The classical attack motivates formal definitions. A successful round trip or a ciphertext that looks scrambled establishes neither secrecy nor a proof.

Chapter 2 · Foundations

Find the assumptions behind perfect secrecy

Direct coverage

Perfect secrecy, one-time pads and their limits

Reading pointers: 2.1–2.4

  • OTP VaultDirect coverage

    Inspect XOR arithmetic, candidate-message keys and the two-time-pad attack.

  • Good RandomnessIntroductory coverage

    Separate a source of unpredictability from deterministic expansion and statistical appearance.

Try this: Keep a ciphertext fixed and derive keys for two different candidate messages of the same length. Then reuse a pad for two messages and inspect the ciphertext XOR.

Explain the result: Which claim fails when the pad is reused? Which assumptions concern the key distribution rather than the XOR operation?

Coverage boundary: Perfect secrecy requires an independent, uniformly random secret pad of the required length, used only once. OTP Vault uses browser-generated bytes to demonstrate the arithmetic; it does not certify perfect physical randomness. The pad alone supplies no message authentication.

Give the attacker an encryption oracle

Direct coverage

Computational secrecy, pseudorandomness and encryption modes

  • AES ModesDirect coverage

    Compare repeated-block leakage and the role of IVs, counters and nonces.

  • ChaCha20 StreamDirect coverage

    Inspect generated keystream and the consequence of repeating a nonce with the same key.

Chapter 4 · Symmetric cryptography

Try to make a changed message verify

Direct coverage

Authentication tags and forgery resistance

Reading pointers: 4.1–4.6

  • MAC RaceDirect coverage

    Compare HMAC, CMAC, Poly1305 and GHASH, including attacks on incorrect use.

  • Poly1305 MACDirect coverage

    Step through polynomial authentication and examine one-time-key reuse.

Try this: Generate a tag, change the message and check verification. In Poly1305 MAC, reuse the one-time key and follow the resulting algebraic attack.

Chapter 5 · Symmetric cryptography

Turn decryption feedback into an attack

Direct coverage

Chosen-ciphertext resistance and authenticated encryption

Reading pointers: 5.1–5.4

  • Padding OracleDirect coverage

    Recover plaintext through a concrete CBC padding-validity oracle.

  • Protocol ComposeDirect coverage

    Compare encrypt-then-MAC, MAC-then-encrypt, encrypt-and-MAC and authenticated encryption.

  • AES ModesDirect coverage

    Observe authenticated decryption rejecting tampered ciphertext.

  • Nonce GuardModern counterpart

    Extend the discussion to misuse resistance by comparing GCM and GCM-SIV.

Try this: Recover bytes in Padding Oracle. Next run Protocol Compose’s padding-oracle panel with MAC-then-encrypt, then encrypt-then-MAC. Identify whether authentication happens before decryption and how that changes the result.

Explain the result: What information does the oracle reveal? Which assumptions make the generic construction secure, and which implementation choices can violate them?

Coverage boundary: A demonstrated implementation attack is evidence of a failure under its stated conditions. It is not a proof about every implementation of that composition. Authentication, nonce requirements and session replay handling remain distinct obligations.

Chapter 6 · Symmetric cryptography

Name the hash property you actually need

Direct coverage

Hash security, domain extension and applications

Reading pointers: 6.1–6.6

  • Hash ZooDirect coverage

    Inspect Merkle–Damgård, sponge and tree structures with actual hash computations.

  • Collision VaultDirect coverage

    Verify collision examples and distinguish equal digests from recovering an original message.

  • Merkle VaultDirect coverage

    Build inclusion proofs and test a changed leaf against the expected root.

  • Commit GateDirect coverage

    Separate commitment hiding from binding and test an attempted altered opening.

  • KDF ChainDirect coverage

Try this: Verify a collision pair, then change a leaf in Merkle Vault. For each experiment, state which object is fixed and which new object an attacker must produce.

Explain the result: How do a collision, a preimage and a second preimage differ? Why does a hash commitment need unpredictable opening randomness as well as an appropriate hash assumption?

Coverage boundary: A concrete hash is not literally an ideal random oracle, and an avalanche picture is not a collision-resistance proof. Consult the author errata when comparing hash properties: second-preimage resistance does not imply preimage resistance without additional conditions.

Chapter 8 · Symmetric cryptography

One-way functions, hard-core predicates and theoretical constructions

  • Hidden BitIntroductory coverage

    Use the PRP/PRF switching game and an ElGamal security reduction to DDH as bridges to formal reasoning.

  • Feistel ForgeIntroductory coverage

    Review round reversibility before reading the distinct theorem about pseudorandom permutations.

Try this: In Hidden Bit, vary the query count in the switching game and inspect the collision bound. Then trace what the live reduction forwards to its adversary and how it translates the answer.

Chapter 9 · Public-key cryptography

Separate arithmetic from a hardness assumption

Direct coverage

Modular arithmetic, finite groups and candidate hard problems

Reading pointers: 9.1–9.4; Appendix B for algorithms

  • Educational RSADirect coverage

    Compute small RSA keys and modular exponentiation, then inspect weak-key factoring.

  • Point ArithmeticDirect coverage

    Carry out group addition and scalar multiplication over a finite field.

  • Curve LensDirect coverage

    Connect curve arithmetic to concrete ECDH examples and parameter choices.

  • ElGamal PlainDirect coverage

    Inspect finite-group encryption and the discrete-log relation behind its keys.

Try this: Follow one small RSA encryption by hand, then use Point Arithmetic to build a scalar multiple through repeated additions. Identify the inverse problem an attacker would face in each setting.

Explain the result: How do factoring, RSA inversion, discrete logarithms, CDH and DDH ask different questions? Which implication would need a proof?

Coverage boundary: Small computations make the algebra inspectable; they do not establish hardness for production parameters. RSA inversion is not known in general to be equivalent to factoring, and discrete-log hardness alone does not establish DDH in every group.

Chapter 12 · Public-key cryptography

Public-key security, hybrid encryption, ElGamal and RSA padding

    Use an adaptive IND-CCA2 oracle that rejects the exact challenge but permits other queries.

  • ElGamal PlainDirect coverage

  • RSA ForgeDirect coverage

Try this: Run Hidden Bit’s ElGamal CCA2 oracle game. Inspect the trace showing refusal of the exact challenge and the related-ciphertext query. Repeat with RSA-OAEP and compare the information returned.

Chapter 15 · Public-key cryptography

Compute on ciphertexts and distribute decryption

Direct coverage

Homomorphic encryption, secret sharing and threshold methods

Reading pointers: 15.2–15.3; read 15.1, 15.4–15.5 for remaining constructions

  • Paillier GateDirect coverage

    Multiply ciphertexts to add plaintexts; inspect rerandomization and modulo wraparound.

  • Shamir GateDirect coverage

    Reconstruct a shared secret from a qualified subset.

  • VSS GateDirect coverage

    Check shares and explore hiding versus binding, including the deliberately knowable generator relation.

  • Threshold DecryptDirect coverage

Try this: Add two encrypted values in Paillier Gate and verify the decrypted total. In Threshold Decrypt, generate a key, ciphertext and partial decryptions. Try recovery with too few parties. About this reading path

Read a chapter, make a prediction, then change one assumption in an experiment. Use what happens to explain the security claim in your own words.

Read → predict → experiment → explain

Independent and unaffiliated with the authors or publisher. The practice prompts are original; no textbook passages or exercises are reproduced. Chapter connections were reviewed on 20 October 2026. Experiments illustrate security ideas; they do not replace proofs.

Optional checkmarks record exploration, not mastery. They stay in this browser when local storage is available and are separate from the Applied Cryptography path.

What the coverage labels mean

Direct coverage A lab addresses a selected concept in that chapter. The label does not promise complete chapter coverage.

Modern counterpart A different practical construction or a later standard explores a related idea. Read the lab note for how it differs from the book’s example.

Introductory coverage A partial bridge or intuition builder. Formal definitions, reductions and unimplemented constructions remain reading work.

Each lab has its own label. The chapter label describes the overall connection; the coverage filter uses that chapter label.
