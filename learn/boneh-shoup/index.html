Chapter 2 · Secret key cryptography

Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Separate perfect secrecy from computational evidence

Read: §§2.1–2.3, especially §2.2.5 · Open chapter in free PDF

Predict before opening a lab: Can one ciphertext be consistent with two different equal-length messages if the secret pad is unknown?

  • OTP VaultDirect connection

    Inspect XOR arithmetic, alternative message/key pairs and pad reuse.

  • Hidden BitIntroductory bridge

    Connect the bit-guessing viewpoint to executable distinguishing experiments.

Try this: In OTP Vault, keep a ciphertext fixed and compare keys consistent with two candidate messages. Reuse a pad for two messages and inspect the ciphertext XOR. State exactly which assumption changed.

Explain the result: What does a probability statement about every possible message require that a finite set of successful encryptions cannot establish?

Coverage boundary: Perfect secrecy requires a uniformly random, independent secret pad of sufficient length used once. Browser-generated bytes illustrate the arithmetic without certifying physical randomness. The pad alone does not authenticate messages; Hidden Bit is a bridge to computational games, not a proof of perfect secrecy.

Chapter 3 · Secret key cryptography

Stream ciphers

Direct connection

Learning focus: Watch repeated keystream cancel out

Read: §§3.1–3.3, §3.6 and §3.10 · Open chapter in free PDF

Predict before opening a lab: If the same key and nonce reproduce a keystream, what remains when two ciphertexts are XORed?

  • ChaCha20 StreamDirect connection

    Inspect keystream generation and the consequence of nonce reuse.

  • OTP VaultDirect connection

    Compare reused pad bytes with reused stream-cipher keystream.

  • Good RandomnessIntroductory bridge

    Distinguish an entropy source from deterministic expansion.

Try this: Run the nonce-reuse experiment in ChaCha20 Stream with two different messages. Compare its XOR relationship with the reused-pad experiment. Explain which inputs must be repeated for the same keystream bytes to recur.

Chapter 4 · Secret key cryptography

Block ciphers

Direct connection

Learning focus: Distinguish reversible rounds from a secure permutation

Read: §§4.1–4.2, §4.4.3 and §4.5 · Open chapter in free PDF

Predict before opening a lab: Can a Feistel network be inverted even when its round function cannot?

  • Feistel ForgeDirect connection

    Step through DES and inspect the Feistel construction and meet-in-the-middle attack.

  • AES ModesDirect connection

    See why applying a block cipher independently to blocks leaks repeated structure.

  • Hidden BitDirect connection

    Experiment with the PRP/PRF distinction at small teaching sizes.

Try this: Follow one Feistel encryption and its decryption, noting the round-key order. Compare repeated blocks in AES Modes. In Hidden Bit, inspect the collision experiment used to distinguish random functions from permutations.

Explain the result: Why are invertibility, avalanche behavior and pseudorandomness three different properties?

Chapter 8 · Secret key cryptography

Message integrity from collision resistant hashing

Direct connection

Learning focus: Match the hash property to the task

Read: §§8.1, 8.3–8.4, 8.7, 8.9–8.10 and 8.12 · Open chapter in free PDF

Predict before opening a lab: Does changing one byte of a file preserve either its hash or its membership proof under an existing Merkle root?

  • Hash ZooDirect connection

    Compare digests and inspect length-extension behavior.

  • MAC RaceDirect connection

    Use HMAC to distinguish a keyed authentication construction from plain hashing.

  • Merkle ProofsDirect connection

    Verify membership against a fixed root, then change the data.

  • Commit GateDirect connection

    Explore binding and hiding as separate commitment goals.

Try this: Hash two nearly identical messages, then verify a Merkle proof before and after changing the leaf. Inspect Commit Gate’s opening checks. For each result, name the property needed rather than saying only “the hash is secure.”

Explain the result: Which attacks concern collisions, preimages, length extension, or a commitment’s hidden value?

Coverage boundary: Avalanche behavior is not evidence of collision resistance. A Merkle proof depends on a trusted root and its encoding. Commitment hiding also needs suitable randomness. The HMAC and random-oracle arguments are reading work; a deployed hash is not literally a random oracle.

Chapter 9 · Secret key cryptography

Authenticated Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Find out when decryption releases information

Read: §§9.1–9.2, 9.4–9.5, 9.7 and 9.10 · Open chapter in free PDF

Predict before opening a lab: What changes when authentication is checked before CBC decryption can reveal padding validity?

  • Protocol ComposeDirect connection

    Compare encrypt-then-MAC and other compositions with actual verification behavior.

  • Padding OracleDirect connection

    Recover plaintext through a CBC padding-validity oracle.

  • AES ModesDirect connection

    Explore the failures of RC4-based WEP with synthetic teaching data.

Learning focus: Agree on bits without assuming an identity

Predict before opening a lab: Can Alice and Bob each complete Diffie–Hellman while sharing different keys with a middle party?

  • Educational RSADirect connection

    Inspect small RSA keys, modular exponentiation and the trapdoor.

  • Key ExchangeDirect connection

    Follow the public contributions and shared secret in an honest exchange.

What the connection labels mean

Direct connection A lab addresses a selected concept in that chapter. This is a connection to part of the chapter, with its scope stated in the lab note and activity boundary.

Modern counterpart A different practical construction or a later standard explores a related idea. Read the lab note for how it differs from the book’s example.

Introductory bridge A partial bridge or intuition builder. Formal definitions, reductions and unimplemented constructions remain reading work.

Supplementary exploration Labs for a topic whose chapter or section is unfinished in the pinned draft. These are independent extensions, not coverage of unwritten text.

Each lab has its own label. The chapter label describes the overall connection and is used by the filter. None of the labels implies complete chapter coverage.
