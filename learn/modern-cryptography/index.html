Chapter 1 · Foundations

Introduction

Direct connection

Learning focus: Decide what “secure” should mean

Read: §§1.3–1.4; §1.2 for the encryption setting

Predict before opening a lab: Which patterns do you expect a short repeating key to leave in the ciphertext, and what evidence would make you revise that prediction?

  • Vigenère BreakDirect connection

    Use frequency and repeated-pattern evidence to challenge a classical cipher.

  • Hidden BitIntroductory bridge

    Preview the challenger, adversary and success condition that later chapters make precise.

Try this: Use a supplied challenge encrypted under a short repeating key. Before opening Hidden Bit, write down what the attacker sees, what the attacker may do, and what would count as success.

Explain the result: Why is “I cannot read this ciphertext” a weaker claim than a definition covering every efficient adversary?

Coverage boundary: The classical attack motivates formal definitions. A successful round trip or a ciphertext that looks scrambled establishes neither secrecy nor a proof.

Chapter 2 · Foundations

Perfectly Secret Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Find the assumptions behind perfect secrecy

Read: §§2.1–2.3; §2.4 for Shannon’s theorem

Predict before opening a lab: For one fixed ciphertext, can two equal-length candidate messages both have consistent keys, and what do you expect to become visible when a pad is reused?

  • OTP VaultDirect connection

    Inspect XOR arithmetic, candidate-message keys and the two-time-pad attack.

  • Good RandomnessIntroductory bridge

    Separate a source of unpredictability from deterministic expansion and statistical appearance.

Try this: Keep a ciphertext fixed and derive keys for two different candidate messages of the same length. Then reuse a pad for two messages and inspect the ciphertext XOR.

Explain the result: Which claim fails when the pad is reused? Which assumptions concern the key distribution rather than the XOR operation?

Coverage boundary: Perfect secrecy requires an independent, uniformly random secret pad of the required length, used only once. OTP Vault uses browser-generated bytes to demonstrate the arithmetic; it does not certify perfect physical randomness. The pad alone supplies no message authentication.

Chapter 3 · Symmetric cryptography

Private-Key Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Give the attacker an encryption oracle

Read: §§3.4.2, 3.5.1 and 3.6.3; §3.6.4 for nonce-based encryption

Predict before opening a lab: Which supplied strategies do you expect to distinguish deterministic encryption, randomized encryption and chained-IV CBC, and why?

  • Hidden BitDirect connection

    Run IND-CPA experiments with supplied adversaries and compare their measured advantage.

  • AES ModesDirect connection

    Compare repeated-block leakage and the role of IVs, counters and nonces.

  • ChaCha20 StreamDirect connection

    Inspect generated keystream and the consequence of repeating a nonce with the same key.

Try this: In Hidden Bit, compare re-encryption against deterministic encryption with randomized encryption. Then select the chained-IV CBC fixture and compare the weak adversaries with the IV predictor.

Explain the result: What changed in the adversary’s information or strategy? Why does a near-zero measured advantage for one strategy leave other attacks open?

Coverage boundary: These experiments address selected chosen-plaintext attacks and mode behavior. Finite trials do not prove computational indistinguishability. The EAV/CPA definitions, negligible functions and PRG/PRF-based reductions still require the reading.

Chapter 4 · Symmetric cryptography

Message Authentication Codes

Direct connection

Learning focus: Try to make a changed message verify

Read: §§4.1–4.2 and 4.5; §4.6.2 for one-time authentication

Predict before opening a lab: Will the original tag verify after the message changes, and will reusing the entire Poly1305 one-time key give the attacker a new way to produce a valid tag?

  • MAC RaceDirect connection

    Compare HMAC, CMAC, Poly1305 and GHASH, including attacks on incorrect use.

  • Poly1305 MACDirect connection

    Step through polynomial authentication and examine one-time-key reuse.

Try this: Generate a tag, keep it fixed, change the message and check verification. In Poly1305 MAC, reuse the one-time key and follow the resulting algebraic attack.

Explain the result: What must a forgery achieve beyond replaying a previously authenticated message? Why does a valid MAC reveal nothing by itself about confidentiality?

Coverage boundary: MAC Race’s CMAC is a related construction, not the chapter’s basic CBC-MAC verbatim. Domain-extension proofs, universal-hashing bounds and information-theoretic authentication are only partly illustrated. Key requirements differ between MAC families.

Chapter 5 · Symmetric cryptography

CCA-Security and Authenticated Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Turn decryption feedback into an attack

Read: §§5.1.1–5.1.2 and 5.3.1; §5.4 for communication sessions

Predict before opening a lab: Will checking the MAC before CBC decryption remove the padding signal used by the attacker, and which result in the trace would support your prediction?

  • Padding OracleDirect connection

    Recover plaintext through a concrete CBC padding-validity oracle.

  • Protocol ComposeDirect connection

    Compare encrypt-then-MAC, MAC-then-encrypt, encrypt-and-MAC and authenticated encryption.

  • AES ModesDirect connection

    Observe authenticated decryption rejecting tampered ciphertext.

  • Nonce GuardModern counterpart

    Extend the discussion to misuse resistance by comparing GCM and GCM-SIV.

Try this: Recover bytes in Padding Oracle. Next run Protocol Compose’s padding-oracle panel with MAC-then-encrypt, then encrypt-then-MAC. Identify whether authentication happens before decryption and how that changes the result.

Explain the result: What information does the oracle reveal? Which assumptions make the generic construction secure, and which implementation choices can violate them?

Coverage boundary: A demonstrated implementation attack is evidence of a failure under its stated conditions. It is not a proof about every implementation of that composition. Authentication, nonce requirements and session replay handling remain distinct obligations.

Chapter 6 · Symmetric cryptography

Hash Functions and Applications

Direct connection

Learning focus: Name the hash property you actually need

Read: §§6.1.1–6.1.2, 6.3.1, 6.4.1–6.4.3 and 6.6.2–6.6.3; Appendix A.4; §§6.5 and 6.6.5 for commitments

Predict before opening a lab: If two different letters have the same shortened digest, will a valid MAC over that digest distinguish them? What changes when a fresh salt is chosen after an inversion table was built?

  • Hash ZooDirect connection

    Inspect Merkle–Damgård, sponge and tree structures with actual hash computations.

  • Collision VaultDirect connection

    Verify collision examples and distinguish equal digests from recovering an original message.

  • Hash HuntDirect connection

    Run birthday and small-space collision searches, transfer a tag in a deliberately insecure hash-then-MAC construction, and compare actual chain precomputation with fresh salts.

  • Merkle VaultDirect connection

    Build inclusion proofs and inspect how a changed tree invalidates a proof checked against its new root.

  • Commit GateDirect connection

    Separate commitment hiding from binding and test an attempted altered opening.

  • KDF ChainDirect connection

    Compare password derivation with extracting and expanding existing key material.

Try this: In Hash Hunt, find a collision at a small output width and independently compare the displayed inputs and digests. Run Meaningful forgery, then the chain-table and salt experiments; separate endpoint matches from verified preimages. For the Merkle-tree connection, retain an original inclusion proof, use Tamper Leaf, and compare its reconstructed root with the changed tree’s root.

Explain the result: How do a collision, a preimage and a second preimage differ? Why must a verifier obtain the expected Merkle root from a trusted source? What role does unpredictable opening randomness play in a hash commitment?

Coverage boundary: Hash Hunt attacks deliberately shortened outputs and an insecure hash-then-MAC composition, not full SHA-256 or HMAC. Its rainbow chains are a later extension to the Hellman connection; probability models and measured outcomes remain separate. A concrete hash is not literally an ideal random oracle, and an avalanche picture is not a collision-resistance proof. Consult the author errata when comparing hash properties: second-preimage resistance does not imply preimage resistance without additional conditions.

Chapter 7 · Symmetric cryptography

Practical Constructions of Symmetric-Key Primitives

Direct connection

Learning focus: Inspect the machinery inside the primitive

Read: §§7.2.2 and 7.2.6 for the activity; §§7.1 and 7.3 for the other labs

Predict before opening a lab: If every round-function output is the same constant, do you expect a Feistel round to remain reversible, and would that make it secure?

  • LFSR ForgeDirect connection

    Explore linear state evolution and why a linear generator alone is predictable.

  • ChaCha20 StreamDirect connection

    Step through add–rotate–XOR operations and the generated stream.

  • Feistel ForgeDirect connection

    Step real DES rounds and run a reduced-keyspace double-DES meet-in-the-middle attack.

  • Biham LensDirect connection

    Follow selected differential attacks at explicitly stated toy and FEAL parameters.

  • Matsui LineDirect connection

    Investigate linear bias and the data an attack needs.

  • Hash ZooDirect connection

    Compare compression-based hashing with the sponge construction.

Try this: In Feistel Forge, replace the round function with the supplied constant-output alternative and undo a round. Then compare invertibility with the statistical evidence shown by a differential or linear attack.

Explain the result: Why can a Feistel network be invertible even when its round function is not? Why does invertibility alone say nothing about resistance to an attacker?

Coverage boundary: The links cover selected mechanisms, not every cipher or hash in the chapter. DES and reduced-parameter attacks are historical teaching examples. Trivium and a complete derivation of every practical construction are outside this route.

Chapter 8 · Symmetric cryptography

Theoretical Constructions of Symmetric-Key Primitives

Introductory bridge

Learning focus: Follow a reduction beyond the experiment

Read: §§8.1–8.8; experiment background: §3.5.1 and Appendix A.4; later reduction: §12.4.1

Predict before opening a lab: At a fixed output length, how do you expect more distinct oracle queries to affect the chance of distinguishing a random function from a random permutation?

  • Hidden BitIntroductory bridge

    Use the PRP/PRF switching game and an ElGamal security reduction to DDH as bridges to formal reasoning.

  • Feistel ForgeIntroductory bridge

    Review round reversibility before reading the distinct theorem about pseudorandom permutations.

Try this: In Hidden Bit, vary the query count in the switching game and inspect the collision bound. Then trace what the live reduction forwards to its adversary and how it translates the answer.

Explain the result: Which object is an assumption, which is a construction, and which is an adversary? What must a reduction preserve about runtime, query access and success probability?

Coverage boundary: Reading-led chapter. The linked labs do not implement the full one-way function → hard-core predicate → PRG → GGM PRF → strong PRP construction chain or its proofs. The DDH reduction is an analogy to the method; DES reversibility is not the Luby–Rackoff theorem.

Chapter 9 · Public-key cryptography

Number Theory and Cryptographic Hardness Assumptions

Direct connection

Learning focus: Separate arithmetic from a hardness assumption

Read: §§9.1.2–9.1.4, 9.2.1–9.2.5 and 9.3.2–9.3.4; Appendix B for algorithms

Predict before opening a lab: Will every base coprime to 561 pass Fermat’s test, and does that mean it must pass Miller–Rabin? Would passing a fixed list of bases prove primality at every size?

  • Prime WitnessDirect connection

    Generate candidate primes, compare Fermat and Miller–Rabin tests, count lying bases and inspect fixed-base counterexamples.

  • Educational RSADirect connection

    Compute small RSA keys and modular exponentiation, then inspect weak-key factoring.

  • Point ArithmeticDirect connection

    Carry out group addition and scalar multiplication over a finite field.

  • Curve LensDirect connection

    Connect curve arithmetic to concrete ECDH examples and parameter choices.

  • ElGamal PlainDirect connection

    Inspect finite-group encryption and the discrete-log relation behind its keys.

Try this: In Prime Witness, inspect 561 at base 2, count all bases for 341 and 561, and compare fixed-base counterexamples with random rounds. Run a bounded prime-generation experiment and compare candidate counts with its estimate. Then use the RSA and curve calculators to identify the inverse problems an attacker would face.

Explain the result: How do factoring, RSA inversion, discrete logarithms, CDH and DDH ask different questions? Which implication would need a proof?

Coverage boundary: Random-base probable-prime evidence is not a primality certificate; deterministic fixed-base conclusions require the stated strict bound. The FIPS generation panel is a later, explicitly limited shape experiment, not textbook wording or standards conformance. Small computations make the algebra inspectable; they do not establish hardness for production parameters. RSA inversion is not known in general to be equivalent to factoring, and discrete-log hardness alone does not establish DDH in every group.

Chapter 10 · Public-key cryptography

Algorithms for Factoring and Computing Discrete Logarithms

Direct connection

Learning focus: Measure how an attack uses structure

Read: §§10.1.1–10.1.3, 10.2.1–10.2.3 and 10.3; §10.4 and Figure 10.1 for parameter choices

Predict before opening a lab: Will equal-size groups have equal attack costs if one order is smooth? Can a field precomputation be reused for a second target, and does the same method apply to a toy curve?

  • Factor ForgeDirect connection

    Run classical methods including Pollard rho, Pollard p−1 and the quadratic sieve.

  • ElGamal PlainDirect connection

    Recover a toy discrete logarithm using baby-step/giant-step.

  • Log ForgeDirect connection

    Compare generic solvers, lift Pohlig–Hellman digits, reuse safe-prime index-calculus relations and independently verify each recovered exponent.

Try this: Keep Factor Forge’s integer attacks separate from Log Forge’s exponent recovery. In Log Forge, compare methods under the displayed equal budgets, inspect Pohlig–Hellman’s prime-power digits, then reuse an index-calculus precomputation for another target. Change to the toy curve and inspect which methods remain applicable; check every reported answer against the independent verifier.

Explain the result: Which feature of an instance helps each algorithm? Why can two instances of the same bit length have different observed attack costs?

Coverage boundary: Log Forge executes Pohlig–Hellman, collision-based discrete-log methods and safe-prime index calculus at bounded teaching sizes. Large digit problems can leave only a partial congruence; a budget stop proves no hardness claim. Its curve is a verified toy fixture, and the field-specific index solver does not apply to it. Number-field-sieve material remains a cost model. Browser timings and key-length tables do not demonstrate a break of deployed keys.

Key Management and the Public-Key Revolution

Direct connection

Learning focus: Agree on a key and identify the peer

Read: §§11.2–11.4; §11.1 for the key-management problem

Predict before opening a lab: Can both endpoints complete a key exchange while each shares a different secret with the attacker?

  • Kerberos v5Modern counterpart

    Use a concrete ticket-based counterpart to the chapter’s key-distribution-center model.

  • Key ExchangeDirect connection

    Follow how two parties derive a shared secret over a public channel.

  • DH MITMDirect connection

    Interpose an attacker in unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman.

  • Key LifecycleModern counterpart

    Extend the chapter’s management problem to rotation, compromise and recovery decisions.

Chapter 12 · Public-key cryptography

Public-Key Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Challenge public-key encryption after seeing the ciphertext

Read: §§12.2.3, 12.4.1 and 12.5.4 for the activity; §§12.3–12.5 for the other labs

Predict before opening a lab: Does refusing the exact challenge ciphertext also prevent the attacker from learning its plaintext through a modified ElGamal ciphertext?

  • Hidden BitDirect connection

    Use an adaptive IND-CCA2 oracle that rejects the exact challenge but permits other queries.

  • ElGamal PlainDirect connection

    Examine probabilistic encryption and multiplicative malleability.

  • RSA ForgeDirect connection

    Compare textbook RSA, OAEP and a PKCS#1 v1.5 padding-oracle attack.

  • Iron LetterDirect connection

    Connect ECIES/RSA-OAEP examples with the separation of public-key and symmetric encryption work.

Try this: Run Hidden Bit’s ElGamal CCA2 oracle game. Inspect the trace showing refusal of the exact challenge and the related-ciphertext query. Repeat with RSA-OAEP and compare the information returned.

Explain the result: Why does forbidding only the exact challenge leave a malleable scheme vulnerable? Which assumptions belong to the KEM and which belong to the symmetric data-encryption component?

Coverage boundary: A rejected OAEP modification defeats that supplied attack; it does not prove CCA security. Hybrid encryption here means public-key protection combined with symmetric encryption, not a classical-plus-post-quantum combiner. The labs do not reproduce every construction or reduction in the chapter.

Chapter 13 · Public-key cryptography

Digital Signature Schemes

Direct connection

Learning focus: Distinguish a valid signature from a trusted identity

Read: §§13.2, 13.5.3 and 13.6 for the activity; §§13.4–13.7 for the other labs

Predict before opening a lab: Does a different valid ECDSA signature on an already signed message win each of the two forgery games?

  • Hidden BitDirect connection

    Compare ordinary and strong unforgeability using two distinct valid ECDSA signatures on the same message.

  • RSA ForgeDirect connection

    Contrast algebraic textbook signatures with RSA-PSS.

  • Schnorr ForgeModern counterpart

    Explore BIP-340’s specific Schnorr instantiation and nonce-use requirements.

  • ECDSA ForgeDirect connection

    Sign, verify and investigate a reused signing nonce.

  • PKI ChainDirect connection

    Follow certificate verification and the trust anchor it relies on.

  • TLS HandshakeDirect connection

    Connect signatures and certificates to an authenticated channel.

Try this: In Hidden Bit, create the ECDSA alternate signature for an already signed message and compare the two forgery goals. Then trace a certificate chain back to the root the verifier trusts.

Explain the result: Why is another valid signature on an old message different from signing a new message? What does verification establish about a person if the public key has no trusted binding?

Coverage boundary: Schnorr Forge uses BIP-340 rather than the book’s exact generic encoding. Signatures do not encrypt messages. Certificate and TLS walkthroughs illustrate selected checks; signcryption (§13.8) and complete protocol proofs are not covered by this route.

Chapter 15 · Public-key cryptography

Advanced Topics in Public-Key Encryption

Direct connection

Learning focus: Compute on ciphertexts and distribute decryption

Read: §§15.2.2–15.2.3, 15.3.1–15.3.3 and 15.4–15.5; §15.1 remains reading-led

Predict before opening a lab: Does a Jacobi symbol of +1 tell you which values are squares modulo a hidden-factor modulus? If Rabin decryption returns a square root, what extra power could that give a chosen-ciphertext attacker?

  • Residue GateDirect connection

    Compute public Jacobi symbols, encrypt bits with Goldwasser–Micali, inspect Rabin roots and turn a precisely defined root oracle into verified factors.

  • Paillier GateDirect connection

    Multiply ciphertexts to add plaintexts; inspect rerandomization and modulo wraparound.

  • Shamir GateDirect connection

    Reconstruct a shared secret from a qualified subset.

  • VSS GateDirect connection

    Explore Feldman share verification and a Pedersen counterpart, including the effect of a knowable generator relation.

  • Threshold DecryptDirect connection

    Connect threshold ElGamal to a local ElGamal/AES-GCM demonstration that also checks partial-decryption proofs.

Try this: In Residue Gate, compare the public symbol with the private CRT classification, encrypt the same bit again, and try the ciphertext flip. Run the canonical root-oracle attack and verify any recovered factors, then compare the redundancy rule. For the other connections, add encrypted values in Paillier Gate and test an invalid selected partial in Threshold Decrypt.

Explain the result: Why can homomorphic malleability be useful for computation yet dangerous for integrity? How does reconstructing a secret differ from decrypting without rebuilding the whole private key?

Coverage boundary: Residue Gate now executes Goldwasser–Micali and restricted Rabin constructions; it does not make them CCA-secure or supply generic trapdoor-permutation proofs. The attack depends on the stated root-oracle contract. Redundancy can refuse a query or accidentally admit a root, and the one-bit Rabin construction has a separate CPA scope. Pedersen commitments and proof-checked partial decryptions extend the chapter’s presentation. VSS Gate deliberately exposes a generator relation that defeats Pedersen binding. All threshold parties share one browser; these labs do not establish distributed-system or election security.

About this reading path

Start with the listed sections in your copy of the book. Write down a prediction before opening a lab, run the experiment, then explain what you observed and which assumptions mattered. Each activity ends with the limits of that connection.

Read → predict → experiment → explain

An independent educational companion by Paul A. Clark, unaffiliated with the authors or publisher. The practice prompts are original; no textbook passages or exercises are reproduced. Experiments illustrate security ideas; they do not replace proofs.

Optional checkmarks record exploration, not mastery. They stay in this browser when local storage is available and are separate from the Applied Cryptography path.

What the connection labels mean

Direct connection A lab addresses a selected concept in that chapter. This is a connection to part of the chapter, with its scope stated in the lab note and activity boundary.

Modern counterpart A different practical construction or a later standard explores a related idea. Read the lab note for how it differs from the book’s example.

Introductory bridge A partial bridge or intuition builder. Formal definitions, reductions and unimplemented constructions remain reading work.

Each lab has its own label. The chapter label describes the overall connection and is used by the filter. None of the labels implies complete chapter coverage.
