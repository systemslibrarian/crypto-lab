      4 · Renew evidence before the old key epoch closes

      Epoch 1 is trusted from 1 January 2029 until, but not including, 1 January 2030; the model treats its private key as compromised at that cutoff. Epoch 2 overlaps from 1 October 2029. Its timely timestamp hashes and signs the complete epoch-1 token, including the old signature, before the cutoff. Verification occurs on 1 January 2031.

That separate procedure is not implemented here.

      What should you conclude?

      • Changing a document breaks the match to its signed fingerprint.
      • Changing a signed time breaks signature verification.
      • A dishonest or compromised authority can sign a false old time with a valid key.
      • One signed Merkle root can cover a batch, but each document still needs the correct inclusion path and root.
      • A successor stamp preserves still-trustworthy evidence only when it arrives before the old assurance fails.
      • A late genuine record and a post-compromise backdated forgery can both pass every signature and hash check. Refusing both is the honest result.

      Where trust enters

      RFC 3161 uses signed tokens, a trustworthy time source, policy identifiers and certificate validation. RFC 4998 adds hash-tree batching and periodic evidence renewal. Linked timestamping and external witnesses address a different problem: preventing an operator from quietly replacing all history.

      This workshop computes real SHA-256 and P-256 ECDSA, and every root, proof, token binding and verdict comes from the current run. The epoch dates are declared teaching inputs. The clock, signer, certificate status, algorithm-suitability policy and checkpoint are local simulations. It creates no independently trusted timestamp, certificate chain, blockchain, durable archive or interoperable RFC 3161/4998 object. Existence evidence does not by itself establish authorship.
